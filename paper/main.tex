When Can Humans Really Intervene?\\

\begin{abstract}
Human-in-the-loop architectures are often treated as if providing an intervention channel were sufficient to preserve human control. This paper shows that such a guarantee is inherently system-level: escalation quality, event base rates, finite supervisory capacity, human processing time, intervention deadlines, and correlated demand jointly determine whether intervention remains practically available. We define effective human control (EHC) as the probability that an oversight-critical event is escalated, reviewed correctly, and acted upon before its deadline. An analytically tractable multi-server baseline yields a deadline-aware control capacity and four consequences. First, some targets are infeasible regardless of staffing because human service time itself imposes a hard ceiling. Second, increasing escalation sensitivity can reduce EHC when additional false alerts create sufficient congestion. Third, in rare-event regimes any fixed nonzero false-positive rate induces a first-order staffing requirement linear in fleet scale. Fourth, average utilization cannot guarantee EHC under synchronized alert bursts. The framework contains the classical fan-out capacity formula as an open-request limiting case. Dimensionless simulations with non-Poisson arrivals and non-exponential service distributions show that the main effects persist beyond the analytical assumptions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Supervisory multi-robot systems seek scalability by delegating routine navigation, sensing, and task execution while reserving exceptional or high-consequence decisions for human supervisors. A common design response is therefore to escalate uncertain or sensitive situations to a human. Yet an escalation mechanism and an override interface do not imply that a human can actually intervene when needed: a critical event can be missed by the referral mechanism, delayed behind nuisance alerts, arrive during a common-cause burst, or require more human processing time than the available deadline permits.

We therefore treat human control as an end-to-end performance property. Let \(Z=1\) denote an oversight-critical situation. We define
\begin{equation}
C=P(\text{successful timely human intervention}\mid Z=1),
\label{eq:ehc}
\end{equation}
and ask when a supervisory architecture can guarantee \(C\ge C_{\min}\).

Our contribution is not a new queueing model, fan-out metric, or false-alarm concept. Instead, we connect escalation reliability, base rates, finite human capacity, and deadlines in one guarantee. This yields four system-level results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a deadline-aware feasibility ceiling and unique alert-rate capacity for a finite operator pool;
    \item an \emph{Oversight Paradox}, in which increasing escalation sensitivity can reduce end-to-end control;
    \item a rare-event scaling law showing that fixed nonzero false-positive rates induce staffing linear in fleet scale;
    \item a proof that equal average utilization can produce arbitrarily different deadline-constrained control under synchronized demand.
\end{enumerate}
We reproduce the classical potential-fan-out formula under an open-request approximation and evaluate robustness under non-Poisson arrivals and non-exponential service times.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Supervisory capacity and fan-out.}
Human--multi-robot supervision has long been modeled in terms of neglect or self-sufficiency time, interaction time, and the resulting number of agents that one operator can sustain. Cummings and Mitchell explicitly modeled interaction waiting, queueing, and situation-awareness costs in multi-UAV supervision \cite{cummings2008capacity}; Savla and Frazzoli treated human task management as a dynamical queue whose service rate depends on workload \cite{savla2012queue}; Powel and Morgansen derived multiserver M/M/c supervisory-control queues for autonomous vehicles \cite{powel2012multiserver}, and Bush introduced chance-constrained pooled fan-out analysis for UAV operator pools \cite{bush2015suave}. These works already establish queueing and probabilistic service constraints as supervisory-control tools. Our guarantee differs by conditioning on genuinely oversight-critical events and composing referral misses/false referrals, pooled deadline completion, human correctness, and intervention efficacy in the same end-to-end quantity. Most recently, Perkins et al. revisited fan-out by separating robot self-sufficiency from the human's estimate of that self-sufficiency \cite{perkins2025fanout}, while a systematic review by Rey-Becerra and Wischniewski synthesizes 44 empirical studies of single-human/multiple-robot supervision \cite{reybecerra2026review}. We use these results as capacity baselines rather than novelty claims; our open-request approximation reproduces the potential-fan-out formula as an algebraic consistency check.

\textbf{Alerts, querying, and human effort.}
Alert design can improve or degrade supervisory decisions. Al-Hussaini et al. evaluate alert generation and tasking suggestions in multi-robot search-and-rescue missions \cite{alhussaini2024alerts}. Recent HITL robotics also explicitly trades robot uncertainty against the cost of querying humans: Banerjee et al. use calibrated uncertainty and human intervention cost to decide when modular robot policies should request help \cite{banerjee2026failure}. These approaches optimize or evaluate when to ask for human input. Our question is complementary: given an escalation policy, finite shared human capacity, base rates, and deadlines, when does the resulting architecture provide a specified end-to-end probability of effective intervention?

\textbf{Formal guarantees and meaningful human control.}
Formal HRC work has already pursued provable guarantees: Zhang and Lin use POMDP supervisory control and model checking for correct-by-design high-level collaboration \cite{zhang2019performance}. Their guarantee concerns controller/task satisfaction under human and robot uncertainty, whereas ours concerns whether an imperfect escalation mechanism and a finite shared human service channel can deliver a time-bounded intervention opportunity. Meaningful human control (MHC) work likewise emphasizes that nominal involvement is insufficient. Verhagen et al. operationalize MHC for variable-autonomy firefighting teams and identify operator overload as a threat to control \cite{verhagen2024mhc}; Prinz and Mathew further translate MHC into HRI interface-design requirements \cite{prinz2026operationalizing}. We provide a quantitative system-level counterpart: a probabilistic guarantee connecting referral accuracy to the finite supervisory channel and the time available to act.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

A fleet of \(N\) agents generates candidate supervisory situations at rate
\begin{equation}
\Lambda=N\lambda.
\end{equation}
A situation is oversight-critical with probability \(\pi\), meaning that safe or intended operation requires a human judgment or intervention before the applicable deadline; this is not synonymous with a robot failure. Let \(R=1\) denote referral to a human. The escalation mechanism has
\begin{equation}
r=P(R=1\mid Z=1),\qquad
f=P(R=1\mid Z=0).
\end{equation}
Under independent thinning, human alerts arrive at rate
\begin{equation}
\nu=\Lambda[\pi r+(1-\pi)f].
\label{eq:alert-rate}
\end{equation}

There are \(M\) pooled operators. In the analytical baseline, alerts are FCFS and human service times are iid exponential with rate \(\mu\). Let \(W\) be queue waiting time, \(S\) service time, and \(D\) the intervention deadline. Define
\begin{equation}
Q_M(\nu,D)=P(W+S\le D).
\end{equation}
If \(h\) is the probability of a correct human response after timely review and \(a\) the probability that the correct action successfully takes effect, then
\begin{equation}
\boxed{C=rhaQ_M(\nu,D).}
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}

Let \(m=E[S]\) and define
\begin{equation}
A=\nu m,\qquad
d=\frac{D}{m},\qquad
\rho=\frac{A}{M}.
\label{eq:dimensionless}
\end{equation}
For the M/M/\(M\) baseline, \(Q_M\) depends on physical units only through \((A,d,M)\). Poisson thinning makes the true- and false-alert streams Poisson, and PASTA implies that a critical alert sees the stationary queue distribution. For \(A<M\), the Erlang-C waiting probability is
\begin{equation}
p_W=
\frac{\frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}}
{\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\frac{A^k}{k!}+\frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}} .
\label{eq:erlangc}
\end{equation}
Writing \(\delta=M-A\), conditional waiting time in normalized units is exponential with rate \(\delta\). Hence
\begin{equation}
Q_M=(1-p_W)(1-e^{-d})+p_WH_M(d),
\label{eq:qexact}
\end{equation}
where, for \(\delta\neq1\),
\begin{equation}
H_M(d)=1-\frac{e^{-\delta d}-\delta e^{-d}}{1-\delta},
\end{equation}
and \(H_M(d)=1-e^{-d}(1+d)\) when \(\delta=1\). We use this closed form for the analytical results and numerical frontiers.

\section{Effective-Control Guarantees}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Feasibility and capacity}

\textbf{Theorem 1 (service-time ceiling).}
For any nonnegative waiting time and any human service-time distribution \(S\),
\begin{equation}
\boxed{C\le rha\,P(S\le D).}
\end{equation}
Thus a target exceeding this bound is impossible regardless of staffing or scheduling.

\emph{Proof sketch:} completion requires \(W+S\le D\), while \(W\ge0\), hence \(\{W+S\le D\}\subseteq\{S\le D\}\).

For exponential service this gives \(C\le rha(1-e^{-\mu D})\). The argument does not require Poisson arrivals, exponential service, or FCFS.

\textbf{Theorem 2 (effective-control capacity).}
For fixed \(M,\mu,D\), the stationary M/M/\(M\) completion probability is continuous and strictly decreasing in \(\nu\), from \(1-e^{-\mu D}\) at zero load to zero as \(\nu\uparrow M\mu\). Therefore every feasible target defines a unique \(\nu_M^*\) satisfying
\begin{equation}
Q_M(\nu_M^*,D)=\frac{C_{\min}}{rha}.
\end{equation}
This immediately yields two design quantities,
\begin{align}
N_{\max}
&=\left\lfloor
\frac{\nu_M^*}{\lambda[\pi r+(1-\pi)f]}
\right\rfloor,\\
M_{\min}
&=\min\{M:C\ge C_{\min}\}.
\label{eq:design}
\end{align}
A coupling argument proves monotonicity: adding arrivals while preserving all shared arrival and service times cannot make any shared FCFS completion earlier. Continuity follows from \eqref{eq:erlangc}--\eqref{eq:qexact}; as \(\nu\uparrow M\mu\), \(p_W\to1\) and the conditional waiting rate tends to zero. Thus the intermediate value theorem gives a unique \(\nu_M^*\). Adding a server cannot delay a completion, so the minimum feasible staffing is also a monotone one-dimensional search.

\subsection{Oversight paradox}

Let \(s\) increase escalation aggressiveness and define
\begin{equation}
\kappa_M=-\partial_\nu\log Q_M>0.
\end{equation}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:main} gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{dC}{ds}<0
\iff
\frac{r'}{r}
<
\kappa_M\Lambda[\pi r'+(1-\pi)f'].
\label{eq:paradox}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:paradox} compares the relative gain in critical-event detection with the congestion sensitivity of the human channel. It establishes the Oversight Paradox whenever the latter dominates: a detector can become more sensitive while the end-to-end probability of timely control decreases. Accordingly, detector and staffing decisions are coupled:
\begin{equation}
s^*(M)=\arg\max_s r(s)Q_M(\nu(s),D),
\label{eq:codesign}
\end{equation}
so the system-optimal referral threshold need not maximize recall or any detector-only score.

\subsection{Rare-event scaling}

\textbf{Theorem 3.}
For \(N\to\infty\), \(\pi_N\to0\), fixed \(f>0\), and any target strictly below the service-time ceiling,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\frac{M_{\min}(N)}{N}\to\frac{\lambda f}{\mu}.}
\label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
The lower bound follows from stability, \(M_{\min}\mu>\nu_N\), and \(\nu_N/N\to\lambda f\). For the upper bound, staff at any fixed \(\rho_0<1\): in the many-server underloaded regime the Erlang-C waiting probability tends to zero, so every target strictly below the service-time ceiling is eventually met. Letting \(\rho_0\uparrow1\) matches the lower bound. Thus a fixed nonzero FPR is a first-order staffing term even when genuinely critical events become rare. The mechanism is the base-rate effect
\begin{equation}
\frac{\nu_F}{\nu_T}
=
\frac{(1-\pi)f}{\pi r},
\label{eq:fpratio}
\end{equation}
which diverges as \(\pi\to0\) for fixed \(f>0\): nuisance alerts can dominate supervisory work even when the detector has high sensitivity.

\subsection{Correlated demand}

\textbf{Theorem 4.}
Let \(b\) exchangeable alerts arrive simultaneously to \(M\) initially idle exponential servers. For a uniformly selected batch alert,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
P(T\le D)\le\min\left(1,\frac{M\mu D}{b}\right).
}
\label{eq:burst-bound}
\end{equation}
Consequently, batch-Poisson systems can have identical mean alert rate and utilization while deadline-completion probability tends to zero as batch size grows. To see the bound, let \(K_D\) be the number of batch jobs completed by \(D\). Even with an initially empty system, \(E[K_D]\le M\mu D\); exchangeability gives \(P(T\le D)=E[K_D]/b\). Residual backlog from earlier batches can only worsen this bound.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate structural claims rather than fit a single deployment-specific parameter vector. A 95\% service level is used as an illustrative engineering target in the main figures; changing the target moves the quantitative frontier but not the theorems or qualitative comparisons. The main axes are dimensionless offered load \(A\) and normalized deadline \(d\). Unless otherwise stated, \(h=a=1\) so that experiments isolate referral and capacity effects; lower application-specific values simply tighten the feasibility ceiling. Analytical curves use \eqref{eq:qexact}. Simulation-only results use 50,000 measured alerts per replicate after a 5,000-alert burn-in, with five independent seeds. We compare Poisson arrivals with fixed batches and a symmetric two-state Markov-modulated Poisson process (9:1 high/low rate contrast, identical long-run mean). Service distributions are normalized to \(E[S]=1\) and include deterministic, Gamma, exponential, and lognormal families. Before the stress tests, we validate the discrete-event engine against the exact M/M/\(M\) expression at four \((A,M,d)\) settings; the maximum absolute discrepancy in deadline-completion probability is 0.0037.

\subsection{Classical fan-out as a special case}

For comparison with potential fan-out, suppose each robot contributes a long-run request rate \(\lambda_{\rm req}=1/(RST+IT)\) and one operator services requests at \(\mu=1/IT\). Under the corresponding open-request approximation, \(N\lambda_{\rm req}<\mu\) rearranges to
\begin{equation}
N<\frac{RST}{IT}+1=PFO.
\end{equation}
Using the mean values reported by Perkins et al. \cite{perkins2025fanout} gives 6.13 and 3.48 for their high- and low-self-sufficiency conditions. This is an algebraic consistency check, not a claim that classical fan-out itself assumes an open M/M/1 queue; finite-source pooled models have also been studied explicitly \cite{bush2015suave}. EHC adds referral errors and a deadline-aware completion requirement to this capacity layer.

\textbf{Mechanism checks.} The ablations behave as the theory predicts. At stable load \(A=0.9\) with one operator, finite \(d=4\) gives \(Q=0.330\), whereas removing the deadline gives eventual completion probability one. At \(N=10{,}000\), otherwise fixed parameters require 2 operators when \(f=0\) but 22 when \(f=0.01\). Finally, keeping \(A=1.2\) and \(M=3\) fixed changes completion from 0.979 under Poisson arrivals to 0.796 under batch-8 arrivals.

\section{Robotics Interpretation}
\label{sec:scenario}

We use variable-autonomy firefighting/search-and-rescue as a neutral robotics interpretation, following recent MHC and alert studies in this domain \cite{verhagen2024mhc,alhussaini2024alerts}. A robot may autonomously navigate, map, or inspect while a referral mechanism flags contingencies such as route blockage, uncertain victim detection, communication degradation, or safety-limit violations. In the model, \(\lambda\) is the candidate-contingency rate per robot, \(\pi\) the fraction truly requiring human judgment, \((r,f)\) the referral operating point, and \(S\) the human triage/decision time.

This mapping also motivates correlated demand. A structural change, smoke/thermal front, communication outage, or shared localization failure can affect several robots at once, generating alerts that are not well represented by independent Poisson arrivals. The batch and MMPP experiments therefore act as common-cause stress tests rather than claims about a particular incident distribution. The systematic review in \cite{reybecerra2026review} shows that empirical HRI evidence is concentrated at much smaller team sizes than our asymptotic experiments; large \(N\) is explicitly used to expose scaling laws, not presented as a typical current deployment.

As an interpretation of the dimensionless axes, \(d=4\) simply means that the intervention deadline is four mean human service times (for example, a 90-s deadline with a 22.5-s mean review time). The theory is invariant to that choice of physical units.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The framework provides structural feasibility guarantees, not a universal cognitive model. Human correctness \(h\), intervention success \(a\), service-time distributions, event prevalence, and burst structure are application-specific and must ultimately be measured in the operational design domain. We also assume identical baseline service-time distributions for genuine and nuisance alerts and workload-independent \(h\); class-dependent service, alert fatigue, and priority scheduling are natural extensions. If correctness deteriorates with workload, congestion effects become stronger rather than weaker.

The analytical M/M/\(M\) assumptions provide closed forms, while the simulation stress tests relax both Poisson arrivals and exponential service. The guarantee should therefore be read as an architecture-level assurance target whose parameters must be calibrated for the intended operating domain, not as a universal staffing recommendation.

The practical implication is a co-design rule: fleet scale, referral threshold, human staffing, and deadline cannot be certified independently. A nominal human-in-the-loop architecture is adequate only if the complete escalation-and-supervision channel meets its required end-to-end service level.

\section{Conclusion}

A right or interface to intervene is not equivalent to an ability to intervene. Effective human control depends jointly on warning quality, scale, human capacity, deadlines, and temporal correlation.

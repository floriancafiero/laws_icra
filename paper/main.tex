When Can Humans Really Intervene?\\

}

\begin{abstract}
Human-in-the-loop architectures are often treated as if providing an intervention channel were sufficient to preserve human control. This paper shows that such a guarantee is inherently system-level: referral quality, event base rates, finite supervisory capacity, human processing time, intervention deadlines, and correlated demand jointly determine whether intervention remains practically available. We define effective human control (EHC) as the probability that an oversight-critical event is referred, reviewed correctly, and acted upon before its deadline. An analytically tractable multi-server baseline defines a human-control feasible region over fleet scale, referral policy, staffing, and deadline, with four consequences. First, some targets are infeasible regardless of staffing because human service time itself imposes a hard ceiling. Second, increasing referral sensitivity can reduce EHC when additional false alerts create sufficient congestion. Third, in rare-event regimes any fixed nonzero false-positive rate induces a first-order staffing requirement linear in fleet scale. Fourth, average utilization cannot guarantee EHC under synchronized alert bursts. Dimensionless simulations with non-Poisson arrivals and non-exponential service distributions show that the main effects persist beyond the analytical assumptions. Finally, we derive a queue-aware referral policy and show on held-out bursty-demand simulations that capacity-aware referral and detector-score scheduling improve timely critical-event service over fixed-threshold FCFS.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Supervisory multi-robot systems seek scalability by delegating routine navigation, sensing, and task execution while reserving exceptional or high-consequence decisions for human supervisors. A common design response is therefore to escalate uncertain or sensitive situations to a human. Yet a referral mechanism and an override interface do not imply that a human can actually intervene when needed: a critical event can be missed by the referral mechanism, delayed behind nuisance alerts, arrive during a common-cause burst, or require more human processing time than the available deadline permits.

We therefore treat human control as an end-to-end performance property. Let \(Z=1\) denote an oversight-critical situation. We define
\begin{equation}
C=P(\text{successful timely human intervention}\mid Z=1),
\label{eq:ehc}
\end{equation}
and ask when a supervisory architecture can guarantee \(C\ge C_{\min}\).

We connect referral reliability, base rates, finite human capacity, and deadlines in one guarantee. This yields four system-level results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a deadline-aware feasibility ceiling and unique alert-rate capacity for a finite operator pool;
    \item an \emph{Oversight Paradox}, in which increasing referral sensitivity can reduce end-to-end control;
    \item a rare-event scaling law showing that fixed nonzero false-positive rates induce staffing linear in fleet scale;
    \item a proof that equal average utilization can produce arbitrarily different deadline-constrained control under synchronized demand.
\end{enumerate}
We further instantiate the feasible-region analysis as queue-aware referral with detector-score scheduling and evaluate it on held-out bursty-demand simulations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Supervisory capacity, allocation, and fan-out.}
Human--multi-robot supervision has been modeled through interaction time, self-sufficiency, and queueing capacity. Cummings and Mitchell model interaction waiting and situation-awareness costs in multi-UAV supervision \cite{cummings2008capacity}; Savla and Frazzoli treat human task management as a workload-dependent queue \cite{savla2012queue}; Powel and Morgansen derive multiserver supervisory queues \cite{powel2012multiserver}; and Bush introduces chance-constrained pooled fan-out analysis \cite{bush2015suave}. Perkins et al. revisit fan-out by separating robot self-sufficiency from the human's estimate of it \cite{perkins2025fanout}, while Rey-Becerra and Wischniewski synthesize 44 empirical studies of single-human/multiple-robot supervision \cite{reybecerra2026review}. Complementary work increasingly treats supervision as an adaptive allocation problem: Galati et al. integrate human intervention into dynamic multi-robot task allocation \cite{galati2023auction}; Akcin et al. allocate supervisors across robot fleets under uncertain connectivity \cite{akcin2025fleet}; Yuan et al. adapt task allocation in heterogeneous multi-human/multi-robot teams under state uncertainty \cite{yuan2025adaptive}; and Smith and Adams estimate supervisory workload to support dynamic robot adaptation \cite{smith2026workload}. These approaches optimize or estimate how scarce human effort is allocated. Our guarantee instead conditions on genuinely oversight-critical events and composes referral errors, pooled deadline completion, human correctness, and intervention efficacy into a single service-level quantity.

\textbf{Alerts, querying, and human effort.}
Alert design can improve or degrade supervisory decisions. Al-Hussaini et al. evaluate alert generation and tasking suggestions in multi-robot search-and-rescue missions \cite{alhussaini2024alerts}. Selective-querying methods trade autonomous performance against human effort: Banerjee et al. learn a contextual-bandit policy balancing task performance and query workload \cite{banerjee2025ask}, He et al. allocate a limited budget of expert calls using policy uncertainty \cite{he2026uncertainty}, and confidence-aware failure recovery incorporates intervention cost when deciding whether a robot should request help \cite{banerjee2026failure}. Beyond robotics, Langer et al. formulate effective human oversight through signal-detection sensitivity and response bias \cite{langer2025oversight}. These methods characterize \emph{which situations attract human attention} or how reliably humans detect problematic outputs. Our problem begins at the shared service channel: given the resulting true- and false-referral traffic from many agents, what fleet scale, staffing, and deadline combinations make timely intervention probabilistically feasible?

\textbf{Formal guarantees and meaningful human control.}
Formal HRC work has pursued provable guarantees: Zhang and Lin use POMDP supervisory control and model checking for correct-by-design high-level collaboration \cite{zhang2019performance}. Their guarantee concerns controller/task satisfaction under human and robot uncertainty, whereas ours concerns whether an imperfect referral mechanism and a finite shared human service channel can deliver a time-bounded intervention opportunity. Meaningful human control (MHC) work likewise emphasizes that nominal involvement is insufficient. Verhagen et al. operationalize MHC for variable-autonomy firefighting teams and identify overload as a threat to control \cite{verhagen2024mhc}; Calvert proposes a domain-general framework for operationalizing MHC in autonomous systems \cite{calvert2025mhc}; and Prinz and Mathew translate MHC into HRI interface-design requirements \cite{prinz2026operationalizing}. We provide a quantitative system-level counterpart: a probabilistic guarantee connecting referral accuracy to finite supervisory capacity and the time available to act.
\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We consider a fleet of \(N\) homogeneous agents. In the analytical baseline,
candidate supervisory situations generated by each agent form independent
Poisson processes with common rate \(\lambda\) (events per unit time).
Their superposition is therefore a Poisson process with aggregate fleet-wide
rate
\begin{equation}
\Lambda = N\lambda.
\end{equation}

A candidate situation is \emph{oversight-critical} when maintaining safe or
intended operation requires timely human judgment or intervention. Let
\(Z\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether a candidate situation is oversight-critical,
with \(Z=1\) denoting a critical situation. We assume that criticality is
independently marked across candidate situations, with
\begin{equation}
P(Z=1)=\pi.
\end{equation}

Let \(R\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether a candidate situation is referred to a
human supervisor, with \(R=1\) denoting referral. The referral mechanism is
characterized by its true-positive and false-positive rates,
\begin{equation}
r = P(R=1 \mid Z=1),
\qquad
f = P(R=1 \mid Z=0).
\end{equation}
Conditional on \(Z\), referral decisions are assumed independent across
situations. By Poisson thinning, the referred critical and non-critical alert
streams are Poisson with respective rates
\begin{equation}
\nu_T = \Lambda \pi r,
\qquad
\nu_F = \Lambda (1-\pi)f,
\end{equation}
so that the total alert rate presented to the human supervisors is
\begin{equation}
\nu
=
\nu_T+\nu_F
=
\Lambda\bigl[\pi r+(1-\pi)f\bigr].
\label{eq:alert-rate}
\end{equation}

There are \(M\) human supervisors serving a common queue of referred alerts.
Any available supervisor takes the oldest waiting alert. In the analytical
baseline, alerts are therefore served first-come first-served (FCFS).
Let \(S\) denote the time required for a supervisor to review an alert and
complete the corresponding human decision or intervention command. Service
times are assumed independent and identically distributed and exponentially
distributed with rate \(\mu\), so that
\begin{equation}
E[S]=\frac{1}{\mu}.
\end{equation}
Together with the Poisson alert process and \(M\) parallel supervisors, these
assumptions define an \(M/M/M\) queue in Kendall notation.

Let \(W\) denote the waiting time from the arrival of a referred alert in the
human queue until a supervisor begins processing it. Let \(D>0\) be the
available intervention deadline, measured from the time the referred alert
joins the queue. Thus a human response is timely when
\begin{equation}
W+S\leq D.
\end{equation}
Any additional actuation latency is assumed negligible relative to this
timescale or included in \(S\).

We denote by
\begin{equation}
Q_M(\nu,D)
=
P(W+S\leq D \mid Z=1,R=1)
\end{equation}
the stationary probability that a referred critical alert completes human
processing before its deadline. Under the baseline assumptions, alert class
does not affect the queue discipline or service-time distribution, so this is
also the stationary deadline-completion probability for a generic referred
alert. To keep notation compact, the dependence of \(Q_M\) on the fixed
service rate \(\mu\) is left implicit.

Let
\begin{equation}
h
=
P(\text{correct human response}
  \mid Z=1,R=1,W+S\leq D)
\end{equation}
be the probability that a timely human response is correct, and let \(a\) be
the probability that such a correct response successfully produces the
intended effect. We assume \(h\) and \(a\) are constant with respect to the
current queue state in the analytical baseline. Defining effective human
control as
\begin{equation}
C
=
P(\text{successful timely human intervention}\mid Z=1),
\end{equation}
the end-to-end control probability is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
C=rhaQ_M(\nu,D).
}
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}

For the exponential-service baseline, let
\begin{equation}
m=E[S]=\frac{1}{\mu},
\end{equation}
and introduce the dimensionless quantities
\begin{equation}
A=\nu m,
\qquad
d=\frac{D}{m},
\qquad
\rho=\frac{A}{M}.
\label{eq:dimensionless}
\end{equation}
Here \(A\) is the offered human workload, \(d\) is the deadline measured in
units of mean service time, and \(\rho\) is the mean utilization of each
supervisor. A stationary queue requires
\begin{equation}
A<M
\qquad\text{or, equivalently,}\qquad
\rho<1.
\end{equation}

For this stationary \(M/M/M\) baseline, \(Q_M\) depends on physical units only
through \((A,d,M)\). Independent Poisson thinning makes both the true- and
false-alert streams Poisson. Moreover, the Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages
(PASTA) property implies that a referred critical alert encounters the
stationary queue-state distribution.

For \(A<M\), let \(p_W\) denote the probability that an arriving alert must
wait because all \(M\) supervisors are busy. The Erlang-C formula gives
\begin{equation}
p_W=
\frac{\frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}}
{\displaystyle
 \sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\frac{A^k}{k!}
 +
 \frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}}.
\label{eq:erlangc}
\end{equation}

Writing
\begin{equation}
\delta=M-A>0,
\end{equation}
the waiting time conditional on having to wait satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{W}{m}\,\bigg|\,W>0
\sim \operatorname{Exp}(\delta),
\end{equation}
while the normalized service time satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{S}{m}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
H_M(d)
=
P\left(
\frac{W+S}{m}\leq d
\,\middle|\,
W>0
\right)
\end{equation}
denote the probability that an alert which has to wait nevertheless completes
before the normalized deadline. It is given, for \(\delta\neq1\), by
\begin{equation}
H_M(d)
=
1-
\frac{e^{-\delta d}-\delta e^{-d}}
     {1-\delta},
\end{equation}
and by
\begin{equation}
H_M(d)
=
1-e^{-d}(1+d)
\end{equation}
when \(\delta=1\).

Combining alerts that begin service immediately with those that first have to
wait yields
\begin{equation}
Q_M
=
(1-p_W)(1-e^{-d})
+
p_W H_M(d).
\label{eq:qexact}
\end{equation}
We use this closed form for the analytical results and numerical feasibility
frontiers.


\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate structural claims rather than fit a single deployment-specific parameter vector. A 95\% service level is used as an illustrative engineering target in the main figures; changing the target moves the quantitative frontier but not the theorems or qualitative comparisons. The main axes are dimensionless offered load \(A\) and normalized deadline \(d\). Unless otherwise stated, \(h=a=1\) so that experiments isolate referral and capacity effects; lower application-specific values simply tighten the feasibility ceiling. Analytical curves use \eqref{eq:qexact}. Simulation-only results use 50,000 measured alerts per replicate after a 5,000-alert burn-in, with five independent seeds. We compare Poisson arrivals with fixed batches and a symmetric two-state Markov-modulated Poisson process (9:1 high/low rate contrast, identical long-run mean). Service distributions are normalized to \(E[S]=1\) and include deterministic, Gamma, exponential, and lognormal families. Before the stress tests, we validate the discrete-event engine against the exact M/M/\(M\) expression at four \((A,M,d)\) settings; the maximum absolute discrepancy in deadline-completion probability is 0.0037.

\subsection{Classical fan-out as a special case}

For comparison with potential fan-out, suppose each robot contributes a long-run request rate \(\lambda_{\rm req}=1/(RST+IT)\) and one operator services requests at \(\mu=1/IT\). Under the corresponding open-request approximation, \(N\lambda_{\rm req}<\mu\) rearranges to
\begin{equation}
N<\frac{RST}{IT}+1=PFO.
\end{equation}
Using the mean values reported by Perkins et al. \cite{perkins2025fanout} gives 6.13 and 3.48 for their high- and low-self-sufficiency conditions. This is an algebraic consistency check, not a claim that classical fan-out itself assumes an open M/M/1 queue; finite-source pooled models have also been studied explicitly \cite{bush2015suave}. Our framework adds referral errors, critical-event conditioning, and a deadline-aware completion requirement to this capacity layer.

\subsection{Literature-anchored supervisory timing envelope}

Al-Hussaini et al. study a human supervisor making repeated tasking decisions in simulated multi-robot search-and-rescue missions \cite{alhussaini2024alerts}. Their missions take about 15--20 minutes, contain approximately 10--15 decision instances, and provide asynchronous robot updates every 1--2 minutes; participants are asked to make retasking decisions preferably within 90 s. These decision instances are not selective referrals in the sense of our model. We therefore use the observed cadence only as an \emph{all-review supervisory-demand envelope}, not as an estimate of the referral arrival rate \(\nu\). Fig.~\ref{fig:case} asks what mean service time would be compatible with a 95\% deadline-completion target if all such decision instances required human review.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_robotics_case_study.pdf}

Under the exponential baseline, a 95\% service level allows a mean review time of only about 24.0 s at 30 decisions/h, 22.5 s at 40 decisions/h, and 20.0 s at 60 decisions/h. This is deliberately not a fit to the participants or alert process in \cite{alhussaini2024alerts}; it is a timing-envelope calculation using only their reported decision cadence and stated deadline.

\textbf{Mechanism checks.} The ablations behave as the theory predicts. At stable load \(A=0.9\) with one operator, finite \(d=4\) gives \(Q=0.330\), whereas removing the deadline gives eventual completion probability one. At \(N=10{,}000\), otherwise fixed parameters require 2 operators when \(f=0\) but 22 when \(f=0.01\). Finally, keeping \(A=1.2\) and \(M=3\) fixed changes completion from 0.979 under Poisson arrivals to 0.796 under batch-8 arrivals.

\subsection{Frontier-derived supervisory mitigation}

The frontier suggests two direct mitigations when demand approaches human capacity. First, a referred alert can be served non-preemptively by descending detector score rather than FCFS. Second, the referral threshold can react to queue congestion. We instantiate a simple \emph{queue-aware referral} (QAR) rule
\begin{equation}
\tau(q)=\tau_0+\gamma[q-M]_+,
\label{eq:qar}
\end{equation}
where \(q\) is the number of outstanding alerts. The threshold therefore becomes more selective only after all operators are occupied and a queue forms.

We test QAR on synthetic two-state MMPP candidate streams with \(\pi=.01\), \(d'=3\), \(M=2\), \(d=4\), and exponential mean-one service. For each policy, \(\tau_0\)---and \(\gamma\) where applicable---is selected to maximize \(C\) on three development seeds at candidate load \(L=14\), then frozen for all held-out evaluation streams. The selected \((\tau_0,\gamma)\) values are \((1.7,0)\) for fixed+FCFS, \((1.6,0)\) for score priority, \((1.5,.30)\) for QAR, and \((1.5,.05)\) for QAR+score priority.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig7_capacity_aware_policy.pdf}
    \caption{Capacity-aware mitigation under bursty candidate demand. Non-preemptive detector-score priority serves higher-score referrals first; QAR adapts the referral threshold only when a queue forms. Parameters are tuned at \(L=14\) on separate development seeds and frozen for evaluation.}
    \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

At \(L=18\), held-out timely-control probabilities are 0.780 for fixed-threshold FCFS, 0.841 for QAR, 0.856 for score priority, and 0.862 for QAR+score priority. QAR also limits backlog: relative to fixed FCFS it reduces the mean across seeds of the maximum outstanding-alert count from 18.8 to 7.0; combined with detector-score priority, it achieves the highest \(C\) while limiting that peak backlog to 14.6. The policy is deliberately simple; its role is to show that the feasibility analysis yields an actionable supervisory design lever rather than only a post-hoc diagnostic.

\section{Robotics Interpretation}
\label{sec:scenario}

We use variable-autonomy firefighting/search-and-rescue as a neutral robotics interpretation, following work on meaningful human control and alert support in this domain \cite{verhagen2024mhc,alhussaini2024alerts}. A robot may autonomously navigate, map, or inspect while a referral mechanism flags contingencies such as route blockage, uncertain victim detection, communication degradation, or safety-limit violations. In the model, \(\lambda\) is the candidate-contingency rate per robot, \(\pi\) the fraction truly requiring human judgment, \((r,f)\) the referral operating point, and \(S\) the human triage/decision time.

This mapping also motivates correlated demand. A structural change, smoke/thermal front, communication outage, or shared localization failure can affect several robots at once, generating alerts that are not well represented by independent Poisson arrivals. The batch and MMPP experiments therefore act as common-cause stress tests rather than claims about a particular incident distribution. The systematic review in \cite{reybecerra2026review} shows that empirical HRI evidence is concentrated at much smaller team sizes than our asymptotic experiments; large \(N\) is explicitly used to expose scaling laws, not presented as a typical current deployment.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The framework provides structural feasibility guarantees, not a universal cognitive model. Human correctness \(h\), intervention success \(a\), service-time distributions, event prevalence, and burst structure are application-specific and must ultimately be measured in the operational design domain. We also assume identical baseline service-time distributions for genuine and nuisance alerts and workload-independent \(h\). The analytical FCFS model is a transparent baseline rather than a prescription: Fig.~\ref{fig:policy} shows that detector-score priority and queue-aware referral can materially improve critical-event service, while the service-time ceiling and detector/base-rate constraints remain. Class-dependent service and alert-fatigue models are further extensions; if correctness deteriorates with workload, congestion effects become stronger rather than weaker.

The M/M/\(M\) model is used only for closed-form analysis; the stress tests relax both Poisson arrivals and exponential service. The framework should therefore be interpreted as an architecture-level assurance method whose application-specific parameters must be calibrated in the intended operating domain. Its design implication is joint: fleet scale, referral policy, staffing, and deadline cannot be assessed independently.

\section{Conclusion}

A right or interface to intervene is not equivalent to an ability to intervene. Timely human control depends jointly on referral quality, scale, human capacity, deadlines, and temporal correlation. The feasibility frontier exposes these couplings and supports capacity-aware referral and scheduling policies rather than treating the human channel as an unconstrained fallback.

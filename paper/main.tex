\begin{abstract}
Human-in-the-loop architectures are often treated as if providing an intervention channel were sufficient to preserve human control. This paper shows that such a guarantee is inherently system-level: referral quality, event base rates, finite supervisory capacity, human processing time, intervention deadlines, and correlated demand jointly determine whether intervention remains practically available. We define effective human control (EHC) as the probability that an oversight-critical event is referred, reviewed correctly, and acted upon before its deadline. An analytically tractable multi-server baseline defines a human-control feasible region over fleet scale, referral policy, staffing, and deadline, with four consequences. First, some targets are infeasible regardless of staffing because human service time itself imposes a hard ceiling. Second, increasing referral sensitivity can reduce EHC when the additional referred traffic, particularly false alerts, creates sufficient congestion. Third, large systems require offered load plus only a deadline- and target-dependent constant safety margin; in rare-event regimes this yields staffing linear in fleet scale with false-positive rate as the first-order coefficient. Fourth, no positive deadline guarantee can follow from average utilization alone under sufficiently synchronized demand for a broad class of service-time distributions. The same monotonicities give a conservative certificate under bounded parameter uncertainty. Dimensionless simulations validate the exact baseline and quantify these effects under non-Poisson arrivals and non-exponential service distributions. Finally, we instantiate a queue-aware referral policy motivated by the capacity analysis and show on held-out bursty-demand simulations that capacity-aware referral and detector-score scheduling improve timely critical-event service over fixed-threshold FCFS.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Supervisory multi-robot systems seek scalability by delegating routine navigation, sensing, and task execution while reserving exceptional or high-consequence decisions for human supervisors. A common design response is therefore to escalate uncertain or sensitive situations to a human. Yet a referral mechanism and an override interface do not imply that a human can actually intervene when needed: a critical event can be missed by the referral mechanism, delayed behind nuisance alerts, arrive during a common-cause burst, or require more human processing time than the available deadline permits.

We therefore treat human control as an end-to-end performance property. Let \(Z=1\) denote an oversight-critical situation. We define
\begin{equation}
C=P(\text{successful timely human intervention}\mid Z=1),
\label{eq:ehc}
\end{equation}
and ask when a supervisory architecture can guarantee \(C\ge C_{\min}\).

We connect referral reliability, base rates, finite human capacity, and deadlines in one guarantee. This yields four system-level results:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a deadline-aware feasibility ceiling and unique alert-rate capacity for a finite operator pool;
    \item an \emph{Oversight Paradox}, in which increasing referral sensitivity can reduce end-to-end control;
    \item a constant-safety-margin theorem at large offered load, with a rare-event corollary showing that fixed nonzero false-positive rates induce staffing linear in fleet scale;
    \item a burst bound, valid for general iid service distributions with finite renewal function, showing that equal average utilization can produce arbitrarily different deadline-constrained control under synchronized demand.
\end{enumerate}
We additionally give an uncertainty-aware feasibility certificate from bounded parameter estimates, then instantiate the feasible-region analysis as queue-aware referral with detector-score scheduling and evaluate it on held-out bursty-demand simulations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Supervisory capacity, allocation, and fan-out.}
Human--multi-robot supervision has been modeled through interaction time, self-sufficiency, and queueing capacity. Cummings and Mitchell model interaction waiting and situation-awareness costs when supervising multiple uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) \cite{cummings2008capacity}; Savla and Frazzoli treat human task management as a workload-dependent queue \cite{savla2012queue}; Powel and Morgansen derive multiserver supervisory queues \cite{powel2012multiserver}; and Bush introduces chance-constrained pooled fan-out analysis \cite{bush2015suave}. Perkins et al. revisit fan-out by separating robot self-sufficiency from the human's estimate of it \cite{perkins2025fanout}, while Rey-Becerra and Wischniewski synthesize 44 empirical studies of single-human/multiple-robot supervision \cite{reybecerra2026review}. Complementary work increasingly treats supervision as an adaptive allocation problem: Galati et al. integrate human intervention into dynamic multi-robot task allocation \cite{galati2023auction}; Akcin et al. allocate supervisors across robot fleets under uncertain connectivity \cite{akcin2025fleet}; Yuan et al. adapt task allocation in heterogeneous multi-human/multi-robot teams under state uncertainty \cite{yuan2025adaptive}; and Smith and Adams estimate supervisory workload to support dynamic robot adaptation \cite{smith2026workload}. These approaches optimize or estimate how scarce human effort is allocated. Our guarantee instead conditions on genuinely oversight-critical events and composes referral errors, pooled deadline completion, human correctness, and intervention efficacy into a single service-level quantity.

\textbf{Alerts, querying, and human effort.}
Alert design can improve or degrade supervisory decisions. Al-Hussaini et al. evaluate alert generation and tasking suggestions in multi-robot search-and-rescue missions \cite{alhussaini2024alerts}. Selective-querying methods trade autonomous performance against human effort: Banerjee et al. learn a contextual-bandit policy balancing task performance and query workload \cite{banerjee2025ask}, He et al. allocate a limited budget of expert calls using policy uncertainty \cite{he2026uncertainty}, and confidence-aware failure recovery incorporates intervention cost when deciding whether a robot should request help \cite{banerjee2026failure}. Beyond robotics, Langer et al. formulate effective human oversight through signal-detection sensitivity and response bias \cite{langer2025oversight}. These methods characterize \emph{which situations attract human attention} or how reliably humans detect problematic outputs. Our contribution couples that referral layer to the shared service channel: given the resulting true- and false-referral traffic from many agents, what fleet scale, staffing, and deadline combinations make timely intervention probabilistically feasible?

\textbf{Formal guarantees and meaningful human control.}
Formal human--robot collaboration (HRC) work has pursued provable guarantees: Zhang and Lin use partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP) supervisory control and model checking for correct-by-design high-level collaboration \cite{zhang2019performance}. Their guarantee concerns controller/task satisfaction under human and robot uncertainty, whereas ours concerns whether an imperfect referral mechanism and a finite shared human service channel can deliver a time-bounded intervention opportunity. Meaningful human control (MHC) work likewise emphasizes that nominal involvement is insufficient. Verhagen et al. operationalize MHC for variable-autonomy firefighting teams and identify overload as a threat to control \cite{verhagen2024mhc}; Calvert proposes a domain-general framework for operationalizing MHC in autonomous systems \cite{calvert2025mhc}; and Prinz and Mathew translate MHC into human--robot interaction (HRI) interface-design requirements \cite{prinz2026operationalizing}. We provide a quantitative system-level counterpart: a probabilistic guarantee connecting referral accuracy to finite supervisory capacity and the time available to act.
\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We consider a fleet of \(N\) homogeneous agents. In the analytical baseline,
candidate supervisory situations generated by each agent form independent
Poisson processes with common rate \(\lambda\) (events per unit time).
Their superposition is therefore a Poisson process with aggregate fleet-wide
rate
\begin{equation}
\Lambda = N\lambda.
\end{equation}

A candidate situation is \emph{oversight-critical} when maintaining safe or
intended operation requires timely human judgment or intervention. Let
\(Z\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether a candidate situation is oversight-critical,
with \(Z=1\) denoting a critical situation. We assume that criticality is
independently marked across candidate situations, with
\begin{equation}
P(Z=1)=\pi.
\end{equation}

Let \(R\in\{0,1\}\) indicate whether a candidate situation is referred to a
human supervisor, with \(R=1\) denoting referral. The referral mechanism is
characterized by its true-positive rate (TPR) and false-positive rate (FPR),
\begin{equation}
r = P(R=1 \mid Z=1),
\qquad
f = P(R=1 \mid Z=0).
\end{equation}
Conditional on \(Z\), referral decisions are assumed independent across
situations. By Poisson thinning, the referred critical and non-critical alert
streams are Poisson with respective rates
\begin{equation}
\nu_T = \Lambda \pi r,
\qquad
\nu_F = \Lambda (1-\pi)f,
\end{equation}
so that the total alert rate presented to the human supervisors is
\begin{equation}
\nu
=
\nu_T+\nu_F
=
\Lambda\bigl[\pi r+(1-\pi)f\bigr].
\label{eq:alert-rate}
\end{equation}

There are \(M\) human supervisors serving a common queue of referred alerts.
Any available supervisor takes the oldest waiting alert. In the analytical
baseline, alerts are therefore served first-come first-served (FCFS).
Let \(S\) denote the time required for a supervisor to review an alert and
complete the corresponding human decision or intervention command. Service times are assumed independent and identically distributed (iid) exponential with rate \(\mu\), with mean
\begin{equation}
m=E[S]=\frac{1}{\mu}.
\label{eq:mean-service}
\end{equation}
Together with the Poisson alert process and \(M\) parallel supervisors, these
assumptions define an \(M/M/M\) queue in Kendall notation.

Let \(W\) denote the waiting time from the arrival of a referred alert in the
human queue until a supervisor begins processing it. Let \(D>0\) be the residual intervention deadline available when the referred alert
joins the queue. Thus a human response is timely when
\begin{equation}
W+S\leq D.
\end{equation}
If an event-level deadline is measured before referral, any fixed or
conservatively bounded detection/referral latency is subtracted from that
budget to obtain \(D\). Additional actuation latency is assumed negligible
relative to this timescale or included in \(S\).

We denote by
\begin{equation}
Q_M(\nu,D)
=
P(W+S\leq D \mid Z=1,R=1)
\end{equation}
the stationary probability that a referred critical alert completes human
processing before its deadline. Under the baseline assumptions, alert class
does not affect the queue discipline or service-time distribution, so this is
also the stationary deadline-completion probability for a generic referred
alert. To keep notation compact, the dependence of \(Q_M\) on the fixed
service rate \(\mu\) is left implicit.

Let
\begin{equation}
h
=
P(\text{correct response}\mid Z=1,R=1,W+S\leq D)
\end{equation}
be the probability that a timely human response is correct, and define
\begin{equation}
a=P(\text{intended effect}\mid Z=1,R=1,W+S\le D,\text{ response correct}).
\end{equation}
We assume \(h\) and \(a\) are constant with respect to the current queue state
in the analytical baseline. Using the EHC definition in \eqref{eq:ehc}, the
chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
C=rhaQ_M(\nu,D).
}
\label{eq:main}
\end{equation}
For a chosen \(C_{\min}\), the EHC-feasible region is the set of parameter
settings satisfying \(C\ge C_{\min}\).

For the exponential-service baseline, introduce the dimensionless quantities
\begin{equation}
A=\nu m,
\qquad
d=\frac{D}{m},
\qquad
\rho=\frac{A}{M}.
\label{eq:dimensionless}
\end{equation}
Here \(A\) is the offered human load, \(d\) is the deadline measured in
units of mean service time, and \(\rho\) is the mean utilization of each
supervisor. A stationary queue requires
\begin{equation}
A<M
\qquad\text{or, equivalently,}\qquad
\rho<1.
\end{equation}

For this stationary \(M/M/M\) baseline, define the dimensionless form
\(\widetilde Q_M(A,d)=Q_M(A/m,dm)\). It depends on physical units only through
\((A,d,M)\). Independent Poisson thinning makes both the true- and false-alert
streams Poisson. Moreover, the Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages
(PASTA) property implies that a referred critical alert encounters the
stationary queue-state distribution.

For \(A<M\), let \(p_W\) denote the probability that an arriving alert must
wait because all \(M\) supervisors are busy. The Erlang-C formula gives
\begin{equation}
p_W=
\frac{\frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}}
{\displaystyle
 \sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\frac{A^k}{k!}
 +
 \frac{A^M}{M!}\frac{1}{1-A/M}}.
\label{eq:erlangc}
\end{equation}

Writing
\begin{equation}
\delta=M-A>0,
\end{equation}
the waiting time conditional on having to wait satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{W}{m}\,\bigg|\,W>0
\sim \operatorname{Exp}(\delta),
\end{equation}
while the normalized service time satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{S}{m}\sim\operatorname{Exp}(1).
\end{equation}
Let
\begin{equation}
H_{\delta}(d)
=
P\left(
\frac{W+S}{m}\leq d
\,\middle|\,
W>0
\right)
\end{equation}
denote the probability that an alert which has to wait nevertheless completes
before the normalized deadline; its dependence on \(M\) and \(A\) is through
the spare-capacity margin \(\delta=M-A\). For \(\delta\neq1\),
\begin{equation}
H_{\delta}(d)
=
1-
\frac{e^{-\delta d}-\delta e^{-d}}
     {1-\delta},
\label{eq:hdelta}
\end{equation}
and for \(\delta=1\),
\begin{equation}
H_{1}(d)
=
1-e^{-d}(1+d).
\label{eq:hdelta-one}
\end{equation}

Combining alerts that begin service immediately with those that first have to
wait yields
\begin{equation}
\widetilde Q_M(A,d)
=
(1-p_W)(1-e^{-d})
+
p_W H_{\delta}(d).
\label{eq:qexact}
\end{equation}
We use this closed form for the analytical results and numerical feasibility
frontiers.

\subsection{Structural guarantees}
\label{sec:guarantees}

The closed form above yields a capacity frontier, but several useful conclusions
can be stated independently of any particular numerical operating point. We
make the four principal claims in Sec.~I explicit below, followed by two
robustness consequences; proof arguments are included so that the guarantees
are self-contained within the paper.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (service-time feasibility ceiling).}
Let $F_S$ be the cumulative distribution function of the human service requirement. For any nonnegative
waiting time $W$, any arrival process, and any scheduling rule for which the
human response requires service time $S$,
\begin{equation}
C\le rha F_S(D).
\label{eq:service-ceiling}
\end{equation}
Indeed, $W\ge0$ implies
$\{W+S\le D\}\subseteq\{S\le D\}$. Thus a target above
$rhaF_S(D)$ is impossible regardless of staffing. In the exponential
baseline, $F_S(D)=1-e^{-\mu D}=1-e^{-d}$. Moreover, in the positive-load
$M/M/M$ baseline every finite $M$ has a nonzero probability of waiting, so
the boundary value itself can only be approached as $M\to\infty$.

\textbf{Theorem 1 (unique effective-control capacity).}
Fix $M,\mu,D>0$. On $0\le\nu<M\mu$, $Q_M(\nu,D)$ is continuous and strictly
decreasing in $\nu$, with
\begin{equation}
Q_M(0,D)=1-e^{-\mu D},\qquad
\lim_{\nu\uparrow M\mu}Q_M(\nu,D)=0.
\end{equation}
Consequently, whenever the required conditional deadline-completion level
$0<q^*=C_{\min}/(rha)<1-e^{-\mu D}$, there is a unique
$\nu_M^*\in(0,M\mu)$ satisfying $Q_M(\nu_M^*,D)=q^*$, and
\begin{equation}
C\ge C_{\min}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Lambda[\pi r+(1-\pi)f]\le\nu_M^*.
\label{eq:capacity-frontier}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\begin{equation}
N_{\max}=\left\lfloor
\frac{\nu_M^*}{\lambda[\pi r+(1-\pi)f]}
\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
For monotonicity, couple a rate-$\nu_1$ FCFS queue to a rate-$\nu_2>\nu_1$
queue by adding an independent arrival stream and assigning every shared alert
the same service requirement. Added work cannot advance a shared alert's
service start, so its completion time is stochastically no smaller. Strictness
holds in the stable interior with positive deadline and service rate.
At \(\nu=0\), \(Q_M\) is understood by continuous extension, equivalently as
the completion probability of a tagged alert entering an empty system.
Continuity and the endpoint limits follow from \eqref{eq:erlangc}--\eqref{eq:qexact},
giving uniqueness.

\textbf{Theorem 2 (Oversight Paradox condition).}
Let $s$ parameterize increasing referral aggressiveness, with differentiable
$r(s)>0$ and $f(s)$ satisfying $r'(s),f'(s)\ge0$ throughout the stable
interior. Define the congestion
sensitivity
\begin{equation}
\kappa_M(\nu,D)=-\frac{\partial\log Q_M(\nu,D)}{\partial\nu}>0.
\end{equation}
Since
$C(s)=r(s)haQ_M(\nu(s),D)$ and
$\nu(s)=\Lambda[\pi r(s)+(1-\pi)f(s)]$, direct differentiation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{dC}{ds}<0
\iff
\frac{r'(s)}{r(s)}
<\kappa_M(\nu,D)\Lambda
\bigl[\pi r'(s)+(1-\pi)f'(s)\bigr].
\label{eq:oversight-condition}
\end{equation}
Thus greater detector sensitivity reduces end-to-end control exactly when its
relative gain in critical-event coverage is smaller than the induced
congestion loss. If \(r'(s)>0\) and the detector receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve
is written $f=f(r)$, the same condition
becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{r}<\kappa_M\Lambda
\left[\pi+(1-\pi)\frac{df}{dr}\right].
\end{equation}
Fig.~\ref{fig:paradox} illustrates this condition for a binormal ROC.

\textbf{Theorem 3 (constant safety margin and rare-event scaling).}
Fix $d>0$ and a target
$q^*=C_{\min}/(rha)\in(0,1-e^{-d})$. Consider any sequence with offered load
$A_n\to\infty$ and staffing $M_n>A_n$ such that the spare-capacity margin
$M_n-A_n\to c\in(0,\infty)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\widetilde Q_{M_n}(A_n,d)\longrightarrow H_c(d),
\label{eq:constant-margin}
\end{equation}
where $H_c$ is given by \eqref{eq:hdelta}--\eqref{eq:hdelta-one} with
$\delta=c$. Moreover $H_c(d)$ is continuous
and strictly increasing in $c$, from $0$ to $1-e^{-d}$, so there is a unique
$c^*=c^*(d,q^*)$ satisfying $H_{c^*}(d)=q^*$. Consequently, if
$M_{\min}(A)$ is the minimum integer staffing meeting the target,
\begin{equation}
M_{\min}(A)=\left\lceil A+c^*+o(1)\right\rceil.
\label{eq:safety-margin}
\end{equation}
To see \eqref{eq:constant-margin}, let $c_n=M_n-A_n$ and
$X_n\sim\mathrm{Pois}(A_n)$. Erlang-C can be rewritten as
$p_W^{-1}=1+(c_n/M_n)P(X_n\le M_n-1)/P(X_n=M_n)$. The Poisson central and
local limit estimates make the added term $O(A_n^{-1/2})$, so $p_W\to1$;
conditional waiting remains exponential with rate $c_n\to c$, hence
$\widetilde Q_{M_n}\to H_c$. These Poisson approximations are locally uniform
for bounded positive $c$, so the finite-load threshold in spare capacity is
$c^*+o(1)$; integer rounding gives \eqref{eq:safety-margin}. Strict monotonicity
of $H_c$ follows by stochastic ordering of $\mathrm{Exp}(c)$.

For a fleet,
$A_N=N\ell[\pi_Nr+(1-\pi_N)f]$, where
$\ell=\lambda E[S]=\lambda/\mu$. If $\pi_N\to0$ and fixed $f>0$,
\eqref{eq:safety-margin} immediately yields the first-order law
\begin{equation}
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{M_{\min}(N)}{N}=\ell f.
\label{eq:staffing-law}
\end{equation}
If in addition $\pi_N=O(N^{-1})$, then the stronger statement
$M_{\min}(N)=N\ell f+O(1)$ holds. Conversely, bounded staffing requires
$\pi_Nr+(1-\pi_N)f_N=O(N^{-1})$; when $\pi_N=O(N^{-1})$, necessarily
$f_N=O(N^{-1})$.

\textbf{Theorem 4 (distribution-free burst impossibility).}
Let service times be iid, independent of scheduling information, with $0<E[S]<\infty$, and let $S_1,S_2,\ldots$ be iid copies of $S$. Suppose the renewal function
\begin{equation}
U_S(D)=\sum_{k\ge1}P(S_1+\cdots+S_k\le D)<\infty
\end{equation}
is finite. Here \(U_S(D)\) is the expected number of services that one
continuously busy supervisor can complete by time \(D\).
Suppose a batch of $b$ exchangeable alerts arrives simultaneously to $M$
initially idle servers. If $K_D$ is the number of batch alerts completed by
$D$, continuously busy servers dominate the actual system and therefore
$E[K_D]\le M U_S(D)$. For a uniformly selected alert $J$ from the batch, let
$T_J$ denote its completion time. Exchangeability then gives
\begin{equation}
P(T_J\le D)=\frac{E[K_D]}{b}
\le \min\left(1,\frac{M U_S(D)}{b}\right).
\label{eq:burst-bound}
\end{equation}
Now let batches of size $b$ arrive as a Poisson process of rate
$\beta_b=\bar\nu/b$. Every system has the same mean alert rate $\bar\nu$ and
the same nominal utilization $\bar\nu E[S]/M$, while the bound in
\eqref{eq:burst-bound} tends to zero as $b\to\infty$; residual backlog can
only worsen it. Thus average utilization alone provides no positive uniform
lower bound on deadline completion under synchronized demand. For exponential
service, $U_S(D)=\mu D$, recovering the earlier $M\mu D/b$ bound exactly.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (uncertainty-aware feasibility certificate).}
Write $Q_M(\nu,D;\mu)$ here to expose the service-rate dependence. Suppose
calibration provides bounds
$\lambda\in[\lambda^-,\lambda^+]$,
$\pi\in[\pi^-,\pi^+]$, $r\in[r^-,r^+]$, $f\in[f^-,f^+]$,
$\mu\in[\mu^-,\mu^+]$, $D\in[D^-,D^+]$, $h\ge h^-$, and $a\ge a^-$,
with $r^-\ge f^+$, so referral load is nondecreasing in prevalence throughout
the box. If
$N\lambda^+[\pi^+r^++(1-\pi^+)f^+]\ge M\mu^-$, the uncertainty box
contains a nonstationary operating point and we set $C_{\rm cert}=0$.
Otherwise every parameter vector in this (possibly conservative) box satisfies
$C\ge C_{\rm cert}$, where
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C_{\rm cert}=h^-a^-\min_{r\in[r^-,r^+]} r\,Q_M\!\bigl(&
N\lambda^+[\pi^+r+(1-\pi^+)f^+],\\[-1mm]
& D^-;\mu^-\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:robust-cert}
\end{equation}
Queue coupling shows that $Q_M$ decreases with alert rate and increases with service
rate and deadline. Hence $C_{\rm cert}\ge C_{\min}$ certifies the entire
uncertainty set. Only the one-dimensional minimization over $r$ remains,
because Theorem~2 shows that referral sensitivity itself need not be monotone.

\textbf{Corollary 1 (finite-source sample-path dominance).}
Suppose instead that each robot's rate-$\lambda$ candidate clock is suppressed
while that robot has an outstanding referred alert. Couple the finite-source
and open systems with the same clocks, referral marks, and service requirements.
Every alert admitted by the finite-source system is then also present in the
open system, which may contain additional work. Under FCFS, the completion
time of every such matched alert is therefore no later in the finite-source
system. Thus the open-system construction gives a conservative sample-path
delay bound for matched admitted alerts under this suppression mechanism.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We evaluate structural claims rather than fit a single deployment-specific parameter vector. A 95\% target is used as an illustrative engineering requirement: queue-only frontiers use \(\widetilde Q_M\ge0.95\), while referral-aware results use \(C\ge0.95\). Changing the target moves the quantitative frontier but not the theorems or qualitative comparisons. The main axes are dimensionless offered load \(A\) and normalized deadline \(d\). Unless otherwise stated, \(h=a=1\) so that experiments isolate referral and capacity effects; lower application-specific values enter multiplicatively through \(C\) and tighten the feasible region. Analytical curves use \eqref{eq:qexact}. Unless stated otherwise, queue-robustness simulations use 50,000 post-burn-in alerts per replicate after a 5,000-alert burn-in, with five independent seeds. We compare Poisson arrivals with fixed batches and a symmetric two-state Markov-modulated Poisson process (MMPP; 9:1 high/low rate contrast, identical long-run mean, normalized state-switch rate 0.12). Service distributions are normalized to \(E[S]=1\) and include deterministic, Gamma, exponential, and lognormal families. \textbf{Synthetic detector model.} Detector-specific experiments use a unit-variance binormal score \(Y\): \(Y\mid Z=0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(Y\mid Z=1\sim\mathcal N(d',1)\); referring when \(Y\ge\tau\) gives \(f=\bar\Phi(\tau)\) and \(r=\bar\Phi(\tau-d')\), where \(\bar\Phi(x)=1-\Phi(x)\) is the standard-normal survival function. Thus \(d'\) is the critical/non-critical score separation in standard deviations. We write \(L=\Lambda E[S]\) for the pre-referral candidate load, so \(A=L[\pi r+(1-\pi)f]\) after referral. Before the stress tests, we validate the discrete-event engine against the exact M/M/\(M\) expression at four \((A,M,d)\) settings; the maximum absolute discrepancy in deadline-completion probability is 0.0037.

\subsection{Classical fan-out as a special case}

For comparison with potential fan-out, let \(RST\) denote robot self-sufficiency time and \(IT\) operator interaction time. If each robot contributes a long-run request rate \(\lambda_{\rm req}=1/(RST+IT)\) and one operator services requests at \(\mu=1/IT\), the corresponding open-request approximation \(N\lambda_{\rm req}<\mu\) rearranges to
\begin{equation}
N<\frac{RST}{IT}+1=PFO,
\end{equation}
where \(PFO\) is the classical potential fan-out.
Using the mean values reported by Perkins et al. \cite{perkins2025fanout} gives 6.13 and 3.48 for their high- and low-self-sufficiency conditions. This is an algebraic consistency check, not a claim that classical fan-out itself assumes an open M/M/1 queue; finite-source pooled models have also been studied explicitly \cite{bush2015suave}. Our framework adds referral errors, critical-event conditioning, and a deadline-aware completion requirement to this capacity layer.

\subsection{Frontier-derived supervisory mitigation}
\label{sec:qar}

The frontier suggests two direct mitigations when demand approaches human capacity. First, a referred alert can be served non-preemptively by descending detector score rather than FCFS. Second, the referral threshold can react to queue congestion. For the score model above, a candidate is referred when \(Y\ge\tau(q)\). We instantiate a simple \emph{queue-aware referral} (QAR) rule
\begin{equation}
\tau(q)=\tau_0+\gamma[q-M]_+,
\label{eq:qar}
\end{equation}
where \([x]_+=\max(x,0)\), \(q\) is the number of outstanding alerts (in service plus waiting) immediately before the referral decision, \(\tau_0\) is the baseline detector threshold, and \(\gamma\ge0\) controls how strongly the threshold reacts to queued work. The threshold therefore becomes more selective only when a waiting queue is already present (\(q>M\)). Queueing itself begins at \(q=M\), when the next referred alert must wait. This switch has a direct queue-state interpretation in the baseline: conditional on \(q<M\), a newly referred alert starts service immediately, whereas for \(q\ge M\) it must wait for \(q-M+1\) service completions. Writing \(W_q\) for its waiting time in that state,
\begin{equation}
W_q\sim\operatorname{Erlang}(q-M+1,M\mu),
\label{eq:state-wait}
\end{equation}
with shape \(q-M+1\) and rate \(M\mu\). Thus \(P(W_q+S\le D)\) decreases monotonically with backlog. The QAR rule keeps the threshold at \(\tau_0\) until a waiting queue is present, then tightens it with additional queued work.

We test QAR on synthetic two-state MMPP candidate streams with \(\pi=.01\), \(d'=3\), \(M=2\), \(d=4\), and exponential mean-one service. For each policy, \(\tau_0\)---and \(\gamma\) where applicable---is selected to maximize \(C\) on three development seeds at candidate load \(L=14\), using 16,000 post-burn-in candidates after a 2,000-candidate burn-in, then frozen. Held-out results average five disjoint seeds with 40,000 post-burn-in candidates after a 4,000-candidate burn-in per load. The selected \((\tau_0,\gamma)\) values are \((1.7,0)\) for fixed+FCFS, \((1.6,0)\) for score priority, \((1.5,.30)\) for QAR, and \((1.5,.05)\) for QAR+score priority.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig7_capacity_aware_policy}
    \caption{Capacity-aware mitigation under bursty candidate demand. Non-preemptive detector-score priority serves higher-score referrals first; QAR adapts the referral threshold only when a waiting queue is already present. Parameters are tuned at \(L=14\) on separate development seeds and frozen for evaluation.}
    \label{fig:policy}
\end{figure}

At \(L=18\), held-out timely-control probabilities are 0.780 for fixed-threshold FCFS, 0.841 for QAR, 0.856 for score priority, and 0.862 for QAR+score priority. QAR also limits backlog: relative to fixed FCFS it reduces the mean across seeds of the maximum outstanding-alert count from 18.8 to 7.0; combined with detector-score priority, it achieves the highest \(C\) while limiting that peak backlog to 14.6. The policy is deliberately simple; its role is to show that the feasibility analysis yields an actionable supervisory design lever rather than only a post-hoc diagnostic.

\section{Robotics Interpretation}
\label{sec:scenario}

We use variable-autonomy firefighting/search-and-rescue as a neutral robotics interpretation, following work on meaningful human control and alert support in this domain \cite{verhagen2024mhc,alhussaini2024alerts}. A robot may autonomously navigate, map, or inspect while a referral mechanism flags contingencies such as route blockage, uncertain victim detection, communication degradation, or safety-limit violations. In the model, \(\lambda\) is the candidate-contingency rate per robot, \(\pi\) the fraction truly requiring human judgment, \((r,f)\) the referral operating point, and \(S\) the human triage/decision time.

This mapping also motivates correlated demand. A structural change, smoke/thermal front, communication outage, or shared localization failure can affect several robots at once, generating alerts that are not well represented by independent Poisson arrivals. The batch and MMPP experiments therefore act as common-cause stress tests rather than claims about a particular incident distribution. The systematic review in \cite{reybecerra2026review} shows that empirical HRI evidence is concentrated at much smaller team sizes than our asymptotic experiments; large \(N\) is explicitly used to expose scaling laws, not presented as a typical current deployment.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The framework provides structural feasibility guarantees, not a universal cognitive model. Human correctness \(h\), intervention success \(a\), service-time distributions, event prevalence, and burst structure are application-specific and must ultimately be measured in the operational design domain. We also assume identical baseline service-time distributions for genuine and nuisance alerts and workload-independent \(h\). The analytical FCFS model is a transparent baseline rather than a prescription: Fig.~\ref{fig:policy} shows that detector-score priority and queue-aware referral can materially improve critical-event service, while the service-time ceiling and detector/base-rate constraints remain. Class-dependent service and alert-fatigue models are further extensions; if correctness deteriorates with workload, congestion effects become stronger rather than weaker.

The M/M/\(M\) queue is the analytical baseline; Theorem~4 is service-distribution independent and the stress tests relax both Poisson arrivals and exponential service. The framework should therefore be interpreted as an architecture-level assurance method whose application-specific parameters must be calibrated in the intended operating domain. Exact point estimates are not required for certification: Proposition~2 converts conservative parameter bounds directly into a lower bound on EHC, while Corollary~1 gives sample-path delay dominance of the open-system construction for matched alerts under a common finite-source mechanism. Its design implication is joint: fleet scale, referral policy, staffing, and deadline cannot be assessed independently.

\section{Conclusion}

A right or interface to intervene is not equivalent to an ability to intervene. Timely human control depends jointly on referral quality, scale, human capacity, deadlines, and temporal correlation. At large offered load, the deadline requirement becomes a constant spare-capacity margin above offered work, whereas synchronized demand defeats utilization-only guarantees; conservative parameter bounds can be propagated into an EHC certificate. These couplings support capacity-aware referral and scheduling rather than treating the human channel as an unconstrained fallback.
